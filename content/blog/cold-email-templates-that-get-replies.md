Cold emails that get replies in 2026 share five traits: they are short (under 100 words), they open with a specific observation that proves you researched the recipient, they make one clear low-friction ask, they read like a person typed them (plain text, no images, no links or attachments in the first touch), and they are sent to a verified address from a properly authenticated inbox. Templates help you get the structure right — but the personalised first line is what earns the reply, so every template below has a {{placeholder}} you must actually fill with real research.

## What makes a cold email get replies in 2026

Inboxes have never been more defended. Google and Yahoo now require bulk senders to authenticate with SPF, DKIM and DMARC and keep spam complaints under 0.3%, so a badly built email often dies before it is ever read — fix that first with our [cold email deliverability guide](/blog/cold-email-deliverability-guide/) or run your domain through the [free deliverability checker](/tools). Assuming you land in the inbox, replies come down to a handful of decisions:

- **Length.** 50–100 words. Your prospect decides in about three seconds whether to keep reading. A wall of text signals "mass mail"; a three-sentence email signals "a person wants something specific".
- **A first line that proves research.** "I saw your recent hire" beats "I hope this finds you well" every time. If the first line could be sent to 500 other companies unchanged, it is not personalisation.
- **One ask.** Not "book a demo, check our site, and connect on LinkedIn". One question, answerable in one line.
- **Plain text.** No banners, no logos, no HTML templates. Internal emails between colleagues are plain text — mimic that.
- **No links or attachments in the first touch.** Links depress deliverability and add friction. Earn the reply first; share the case study when they ask.
- **Relevance over cleverness.** The best cold email is a boring email to exactly the right person about exactly their problem. If you are UK-based, also make sure your targeting and opt-out handling comply with PECR — see [is cold email legal in the UK](/blog/is-cold-email-legal-uk/).

## Subject lines: lowercase and boring wins

Counter-intuitively, the subject lines that get opened look like they came from a colleague, not a marketer. Internal emails have subjects like "quick question" or "hiring plans" — two to four words, lowercase, no punctuation gymnastics. Clickbait subjects ("You're losing £10k/month!!") pattern-match to spam and get you reported, which damages your domain for every future send.

Good patterns:

- `quick question` / `quick question, {{first_name}}`
- `{{their_company}} + {{your_company}}`
- `{{competitor}} vs you`
- `your {{job_title}} hire`
- `{{city}} founders`

Avoid: ALL CAPS, "RE:" or "FW:" fakery (it starts the relationship with a lie), emojis, and anything over ~6 words. The subject's only job is to get the email opened without over-promising what is inside.

## 8 cold email templates that get replies

Fill every placeholder with real research. A perfect template with a lazy first line performs worse than a mediocre template with a genuinely specific one.

### 1. The specific-observation opener

```
Subject: {{their_company}}'s {{specific_thing}}

Hi {{first_name}},

Noticed {{specific_observation — e.g. "you've opened a second site in Leeds"
/ "you're hiring two SDRs" / "your reviews mention slow quotes"}}.

Usually when {{type_of_company}} do that, {{specific_problem}} starts
biting — {{one_line_consequence}}.

We help {{similar_companies}} {{outcome_in_plain_english}}. Worth a
look, or is {{problem}} not a priority right now?

{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** the first line is unfakeable proof you looked at their business, which buys you the next two sentences. The closing question gives an easy out ("not a priority"), which paradoxically makes people more willing to answer honestly — including with a yes.

### 2. Problem–agitate–solve

```
Subject: {{problem_area}} at {{their_company}}

Hi {{first_name}},

Most {{job_title}}s at {{company_type}} tell us {{specific_problem}} —
and that it quietly costs them {{consequence — time, deals, hires}}.

If that rings true, the fix is usually {{one_line_approach}}, which is
exactly what we do for firms like {{similar_company_type}}.

Open to a 15-minute look at how that would work for {{their_company}}?

{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** naming the problem in the prospect's own language does the selling for you — if it lands, they feel understood before you've pitched anything. The agitation is one clause, not a paragraph; overdoing it reads as manipulative.

### 3. The trigger event

```
Subject: congrats on {{trigger}}

Hi {{first_name}},

Saw {{trigger_event — funding round, new office, product launch, award,
senior hire}}. Congrats — genuinely.

{{trigger}} usually means {{new_pressure — e.g. "aggressive pipeline
targets" / "more inbound than the team can handle"}}. If so, we help
{{company_type}} {{outcome}} without {{common_cost}}.

Worth a quick chat while you're planning {{next_phase}}?

{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** a trigger event gives you both a reason to write *now* and a plausible new problem the prospect actually has. Timing does half the work — the same email sent three months later is just noise.

### 4. The referral / mutual-thread opener

```
Subject: {{mutual_point}}

Hi {{first_name}},

{{connection — "Saw your comment on X's post about hiring" / "We both
work with {{shared_partner}}" / "{{colleague}} suggested you're the
right person for this"}}.

Reason I'm writing: we help {{company_type}} with {{problem}}, and
{{their_company}} looks like a fit because {{one_specific_reason}}.

Are you the right person to ask, or should I speak to someone else?

{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** any legitimate shared context — a mutual connection, a post they wrote, a shared partner — moves you out of the "total stranger" bucket. The "right person?" close is easy to answer and often gets you a warm internal forward, which is worth more than a reply.

### 5. The competitor comparison

```
Subject: {{competitor}} question

Hi {{first_name}},

{{competitor_1}} and {{competitor_2}} both {{specific_thing_they_do —
"publish response-time guarantees" / "offer instant online quotes"}}.
Couldn't see the same on {{their_company}}'s site — deliberate, or a gap?

If it's a gap, we've helped {{similar_companies}} close it in
{{rough_timeframe}}. Happy to share how.

{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** nothing gets a founder's attention like their competitors' names. You are not insulting their business — you are showing them a specific, checkable gap. Keep the tone curious ("deliberate, or a gap?") rather than smug, and only use this when the gap is real.

### 6. The question-only ultra-short

```
Subject: quick question

Hi {{first_name}} — how are you currently handling {{specific_process —
"outbound prospecting" / "after-hours enquiries" / "invoice chasing"}}
at {{their_company}}?

Asking because we've built something that {{one_line_outcome}} for
{{company_type}}, and I'm trying to work out if it's relevant to you.

{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** two sentences, one honest question, zero pitch-speak. The transparency ("I'm trying to work out if it's relevant") disarms the standard defence against salespeople. This template lives or dies on whether the process you name is one they actually run.

### 7. The follow-up bump (send 3–4 days after touch one)

```
Subject: (reply in the same thread)

Hi {{first_name}},

Following up on the below — one thing I should have led with:
{{new_piece_of_value — a relevant insight, a specific result with a
similar firm, an answer to the objection they're probably thinking}}.

Still worth a quick look, or shall I leave it?

{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** replying in-thread keeps your context one scroll away, and adding something new means you are not just nagging — "just bumping this" adds nothing and trains people to ignore you. A meaningful share of all cold-email replies come from follow-ups, not the first touch, so skipping this step throws pipeline away.

### 8. The break-up email (final touch)

```
Subject: (reply in the same thread)

Hi {{first_name}},

I'll take the hint and close this off — clearly {{problem}} isn't a
priority right now, and that's fair.

If it moves up the list, {{one_line_of_what_you_do}} and I'd be glad
to pick this up. Either way, good luck with {{something_specific_and
_genuine — their launch, their hiring, their busy season}}.

{{your_name}}
```

**Why it works:** the break-up email removes all pressure, and removing pressure is precisely what makes fence-sitters finally reply — it is consistently one of the highest-reply emails in any sequence. The genuine well-wish at the end leaves the door open for six months' time.

For a straight meeting-ask variant, take template 1 or 2 and swap the close for: *"Do you have 15 minutes Tuesday or Thursday afternoon? If it's not useful, I'll say so in the first five."* Offering two concrete slots converts better than "let me know if you're interested", and the promise to self-disqualify lowers the perceived cost of saying yes.

## Follow-up cadence: 3–4 touches, value each time

One email is not a campaign. A sensible cadence for UK B2B:

| Touch | Timing | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Day 1 | Opener (templates 1–6) |
| 2 | Day 4–5 | Bump with new value (template 7) |
| 3 | Day 9–10 | Different angle — new problem framing or proof point |
| 4 | Day 15–18 | Break-up (template 8) |

Stop immediately when someone replies — even with a no — and honour opt-outs without exception. Going beyond four or five touches produces more spam complaints than meetings, and complaints damage deliverability for your whole domain. ## Do and don't: quick reference

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep it under 100 words | Send 300-word essays about your company |
| Open with a researched, specific first line | Open with "I hope this email finds you well" |
| One clear ask, answerable in one line | Stack three CTAs in one email |
| Plain text, sent from a real person's inbox | HTML templates with logos and banners |
| Lowercase, 2–4 word subject lines | Clickbait, ALL CAPS, fake "RE:" subjects |
| Verify every address before sending | Blast unverified lists and eat the bounces |
| Follow up 3–4 times, adding value each touch | Send "just bumping this" five times |
| Stop on reply; honour opt-outs instantly | Keep sequencing people who said no |

## Frequently asked questions

### How long should a cold email be?

Between 50 and 100 words. Prospects skim-read on mobile and decide within seconds whether to reply, so every sentence must earn its place: one researched observation, one line of relevance, one clear ask. If your email needs a scroll, it is too long.

### What is the best subject line for a cold email?

Short, lowercase and internal-looking — two to four words such as "quick question" or "{{company}} + {{your company}}". Subject lines that look like a colleague wrote them outperform clickbait, which pattern-matches to spam and invites complaints that damage your sender reputation.

### How many times should I follow up on a cold email?

Three to four touches over roughly two to three weeks, each adding something new — an insight, a proof point, or a different angle on the problem. A large share of replies come from follow-ups rather than the first email, but going past four or five touches mostly generates spam complaints. Always stop when someone replies or opts out.

### Should I include links or attachments in a cold email?

Not in the first touch. Links and attachments reduce deliverability with many spam filters and add friction before you have earned any trust. Send plain text, get the reply first, and share your case study or deck once the prospect asks for it.

### Do cold email templates actually work?

Templates work as structure, not as finished emails. The frameworks on this page — specific observation, trigger event, break-up — are proven, but the reply comes from the personalised details you fill in. A template sent verbatim to 500 people is just spam with better formatting.

### Is sending cold email templates legal in the UK?

Yes, for business-to-business outreach. Under UK GDPR and PECR you can email corporate subscribers on a legitimate-interests basis, provided you identify yourself, make opting out easy, and honour opt-outs immediately.
